<p>Most qualified B2B sales conversations start from a pain point or problem.</p>

<p>Sales problems. Revenue problems. Operational inefficiency. Budget pressure. People and performance issues. Customer experience issues.</p>

<p>That is where real conversations begin.</p>

<p>Yet when I analyse sales calls, most of them start with the wrong thing.</p>

<div class="section-divider"></div>

<h2>Most Sales Calls Start With the Seller</h2>

<p>The majority of poor calls begin with some version of this:</p>

<ul>
    <li>Features and benefits.</li>
    <li>A product pitch.</li>
    <li>A boast about who the company already works with.</li>
    <li>No context to the prospect or their role in the business.</li>
</ul>

<p>Pitch, pitch, pitch. Convince, beg, and plead.</p>

<p>"Let's just sit down for a coffee. I promise there will be loads of value."</p>

<p>That is not a compelling business reason to meet. It is the salesperson trying to make their problem the prospect's problem.</p>

<p>When it does not work, the team complains that cold calling is dead.</p>

<p>It is not dead. It never will be.</p>

<p>The call is just starting in the wrong place.</p>

<h2>Start With the Problem the Prospect Understands</h2>

<p>Sales is the art of communication.</p>

<p>When calling, you need to articulate the problems you solve in a way the prospect understands. Not in the way your brochure explains it. Not in the way your product team describes it. Not in the way your marketing deck phrases it.</p>

<p>In the way the prospect experiences it.</p>

<p>If you sell sales training, the problem is not "we offer bespoke workshops."</p>

<p>The problem might be:</p>

<ul>
    <li>Your reps are making calls but not creating enough qualified meetings.</li>
    <li>Your team is relying on inbound leads and avoiding outbound.</li>
    <li>Your SDRs are pitching features before they understand the buyer's problem.</li>
    <li>Your managers are measuring dials and meetings without knowing whether calls are improving.</li>
</ul>

<p>Now the conversation has somewhere to go.</p>

<p>This is why I keep coming back to <a href="/blog/lead-cold-calls-with-problems/">leading cold calls with problems</a>. The prospect does not care about your product until they recognise a problem your product can help fix.</p>

<h2>If the Problem Is Not There, Move On</h2>

<p>Problem-led selling is not about pretending every prospect has pain.</p>

<p>That is just another version of forcing the pitch.</p>

<p>If the prospect does not have the problem, probe a little and move on. Do not beg. Do not keep pushing features. Do not try to manufacture urgency where none exists.</p>

<p>If they do have the problem, ask questions about how deep it goes.</p>

<p>How long has it been happening? What has it cost them? What have they tried? Why has it not been fixed? What happens if nothing changes?</p>

<p>That is where qualification begins.</p>

<h2>Qualified Meetings Come From Problem Recognition</h2>

<p>A meeting only matters when there is a reason for it to exist.</p>

<p>That reason is usually a problem the prospect understands, cares about, and wants to explore fixing.</p>

<p>If your team cannot explain the problems they solve in plain business language, they will keep defaulting to features, proof points, and awkward meeting requests.</p>

<p>It really can be simple. Lead with a relevant problem. If it exists, go deeper. If it does not, move on.</p>
